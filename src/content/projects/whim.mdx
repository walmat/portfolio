Whim lets you rent premium products by the month from the brands that build them. You start where
you were already shopping: Whim turns up as an option on a partner brand's own site, and one
monthly price puts the thing on your doorstep — a projector, an espresso machine, a composter, a
pool. From then on you can swap it, upgrade it, have it replaced if it breaks, or request a quote
and keep it.

That shape decides most of what the frontend has to be. Whim's first appearance is on somebody
else's site. Everything after that lives in a portal: what you're renting now, what you've sent
back, and a shop for finding the next thing. Fragile is the company, Whim is the product, and I
owned the frontend.

## The site

The marketing site carries the whole proposition before anyone has agreed to anything. The hero
scatters real product cutouts — a projector, an espresso machine, a pool, a PC case, a license
plate — loosely around the headline, so the page reads the way its headline does: stuff you've
been eyeing, not a product grid.

## The portal

Once you're renting something, the portal is Whim. Four things in the sidebar — Home, Shop,
Payments, Account — and Home opens on what you currently have out, with everything you've since
sent back tucked behind a disclosure rather than deleted. That distinction matters more here than
in a normal store: an order you completed is history, but a rental you're holding is a live thing
with a monthly charge attached, so the two can't look alike.

An order opens under the brand it came from. Whim stays the mechanism; the brand stays the name on
the thing you're renting. Underneath sit the details a subscription needs and a purchase never
does — the date it started, the terms you agreed to, any code applied at signup.

The shop is the newer half. Every card leads with a monthly rate and the retail price it stands in
for — $189/mo against $2,999 for an Eight Sleep Pod 5, $34/mo against $499 for a Reencle composter
— plus the brand that makes it, because the brand is the reason you'd trust renting it. Alongside
them run the three promises that make a rental defensible at all: cancel anytime, free shipping
both ways, replacements at no cost.

The product pages are where renting stops resembling buying. Picking a bigger Pod doesn't show you
a different price, it shows you a difference — King is +$10.00/mo, Full is −$20.00/mo — because
the thing being chosen isn't a total, it's a rate you'll keep paying until you send it back.
